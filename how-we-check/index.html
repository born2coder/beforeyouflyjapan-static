OUR METHOD

How we research a route and calculate its timing

The number that matters is not how many hours remain before takeoff. It is the time left after reaching the correct terminal, collecting bags and protecting the airline procedures. Our guides explain the choices; the planner works backwards through an authored route.

Sources we prioritize

  • Airports for terminal layouts, departure procedures and terminal transfers.
  • Airlines for their own check-in, bag-drop, boarding and assistance requirements.
  • Railways and other transport operators for routes, station approaches and published travel information.
  • Attractions, museums, markets and their operators for admission, access, prices and closure notices.
  • Local government and official tourism organisations for district context and maps.

A source link should answer a particular question. A railway's quoted train ride does not automatically include the walk to its platform. A temple hall's hours do not establish the hours of neighbouring shops. If a price or route detail is not confirmed, we keep the claim conditional rather than filling the gap with a guess.

What the timing includes

Each plan is a sequence of stages. Depending on the route, those stages include reaching the visit area, the visit itself, a meal or break, luggage drop or retrieval, station access, waiting, transfers, the airport journey and movement inside the terminal. They are planning allowances, not live train departures or measured queues.

The airport-ready target is the point at which you can begin the required airline procedures at the correct terminal, with bags collected. The planner currently protects three hours before an international departure. This is our conservative default, not an instruction issued by every airline. An earlier airline instruction takes priority; the planner does not automatically read your airline's rules.

An example of the arithmetic

For a 21:00 flight, the three-hour model gives an 18:00 airport-ready target. If a complete journey takes an illustrative 90 minutes and a bag pickup takes 20 more, the last activity must end by 16:10 at the pickup location. A separate walk or detour to that location must finish earlier. The example is arithmetic, not a published timetable.

A short local visit and the complete airport plan are different figures. “45–60 minutes at the temple” does not mean that a flight in an hour is feasible. The guide keeps the local visit estimate separate from the model that includes transport.

How a plan is reviewed

  1. Identify a named visit, a usable station approach and a return corridor.
  2. Read the relevant official source and record the scope and date of that check.
  3. Assign stage allowances and calculate the complete route backwards from the airport-ready target.
  4. Check that the visiting stage fits the available opening information and closure rules.
  5. Exercise starting areas, luggage choices, walking needs, date boundaries and urgent cases in software tests.
  6. Compare the displayed duration, example stages and personalised results, including the page a traveller opens directly.

The scenario checks are simulated. They can reveal arithmetic errors, impossible opening-hour combinations and missing allowances; they do not measure real traveller satisfaction, actual train punctuality or a route's step-free condition.

Opening hours and uncertain dates

Venue-specific rules are used where they are available. Some general shopping stages use a conservative district window, which is not a promise that a chosen shop is open throughout it. A museum that only opens during exhibitions needs a checked calendar; an unverified future date should not be treated as an available visit.

A verified source fact, an editorial estimate and a simulated test answer different questions. “Reviewed” means that the stated source or model was checked on the date shown. It does not mean an editor recently visited every stop or that conditions cannot change.

Our risk labels

Time risk describes how queues, walking and transfers can reduce spare time. Luggage risk describes storage searches or return detours. Transfer complexity concerns station changes, terminal changes and route navigation. These are editorial judgements for comparison, not measured probabilities.

Correction policy

When an official notice or a reproducible error changes the advice, we revise the affected route or withdraw the recommendation. A shared change, such as a terminal transfer or fee, can require several guides to be updated. A route that no longer fits should lead back to the planner, with an explanation and a simpler option where one is supported.
